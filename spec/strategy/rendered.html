Section 5What the market says

From the research done 26 July — the part worth re-reading, because it corrected a belief we'd both been building on.

"Nobody tracks cost basis" was true in 2022 and is false in 2026. Slabfy, Whuppit, CardLogx, Viewible, InVelocity, CardZen and Mascot all attack cost basis and margin, at $7–40/mo. It is table stakes for the segment.

The wedge that survived scrutiny is narrower and better: per-card inventory that closes into a real double-entry general ledger, across multiple legal entities, with every number traceable to a document. Of ~15 products surveyed, none advertises a QuickBooks/Xero/Zoho integration and none mentions journal entries or a chart of accounts. The reseller accounting tools that do have real books have no concept of a card, a purchase lot, a grading cost line or a cert number. CPAs writing for this hobby still recommend a spreadsheet — that's the real competitive baseline.

Multi-entity is the sharp edge nobody else has. Zero of eight products checked let one operator keep two businesses' inventory and books apart. You built it reflexively because you run several businesses.

What the research says not to build

  • AvoidScanning and price guides as the headline
    eBay's own price guide is free, in-app, scans to the exact parallel, and added portfolios in June 2026. The venue where cards sell is giving away the layer others charge $10/mo for. Scanning must be good enough not to lose the deal; it can never be why you win.
  • AvoidA marketplace, or holding anyone's money or cards
    Competitors' worst reviews are payment and shipping disputes — and those reviewers abandon the tracker too.
  • AvoidA sales-tax engine or nexus determination
    Jurisdictional quicksand, and it violates the posture that has kept this product honest. Record and flag; never compute an obligation.
  • AvoidSet registry / completion / census
    Needs a catalog of what exists. card_identities is a catalog of what has been seen. Unbridgeable without licensed checklist data.
  • AvoidAny projected future value, or "cards as an asset class" framing
    Also a dead commercial category — Collectable wound down Nov 2024, Dibbs Mar 2023, Mythic Markets 2021. Report what happened, never what will.
  • AvoidGrade estimates shown to a buyer
    Internal EV is the entire point. The moment "likely PSA 9" appears on a showcase or in listing copy it's a representation to a buyer about a third party's future act, using PSA's marks. Every card that returns lower is a dispute.
  • AvoidA tax package that computes tax
    No rate applied, no form line numbers as headers, no short/long label — raw day counts, and classification shown as "as classified by you on <date>: <reason>". A once-a-year artifact used under deadline by someone whose CPA disagrees generates urgent March tickets forever.
  • AvoidAn AI-grading accuracy race
    Photo quality matters more than the model. The defensible thing is the capture standard — edge detection, deskew, sharpness gating, templates, retained originals with crop geometry. Market the standard and the evidence trail; never claim a percentage you can't source.

Section 6What to build, ranked

Revised after a 2026 landscape pass that read the actual source and corrected four things — including my own first recommendation, which turned out to be blocked.

The one thing

  • 2–3 weeksA forward money engine — “what you keep”
    Net proceeds, break-even ask, and answer-this-offer, computed from basis that traces to a real row.

    Every money number in the app today is written after the sale. The only forward-looking one in the entire codebase is suggestedListPrice() — max(market, landed_cost × 1.15). A hardcoded 15% that knows nothing about eBay's 13.25% final value fee, the per-order fee, promoted listing rates, or which shipping service a card qualifies for.

    It's the question actually asked all day — not what is this worth (eBay answers that free now) but an offer came in at $340, do I take it. It's the only feature that spends the moat at the moment of decision: a break-even that decomposes into “lot #7 draw $88 + grading $32 + shipping-in $4” is a sentence no competitor can produce, because none of them have a basis they could defend.

    And the structural trick — the same pure function renders the forecast and feeds cardSaleLines(), so after the sale it can show “forecast net $412, actual $407, delta $5 — fee tier changed.” That line requires the forecast and the booked journal entry to come out of one code path. It is the clearest possible proof the numbers are real.

    No vendor, no API, no interlock. It must never pick the price, and it refuses to render a break-even at all where basis_entered is false — that card gets “no stated basis”, never a floor of $0.

Section 7The uncomfortable part, restated

The research's conclusion was that the variety is the trap. The casual collector is served free by eBay; the flipper lane filled in 2025–26 at under $10/mo; the high-end investor needs population and census data CardOps can't source. The winnable market is small and rich — dealers who have crossed into being a business, with an LLC, a CPA, and increasingly consignors whose money is mixed with theirs.

Your own answer differed, and may be better: Claude-style metered pricing, mostly free up to a level of compute and storage, pay when you exceed it. That reaches the same place by a different route — a dealer at volume consumes real compute and storage and pays accordingly, a hobbyist costs nothing and pays nothing. It also fits what's already built: the credit ledger, per-run cost metering, per-photo byte accounting. Worth holding the tension rather than resolving it early.
